# Capita flyer — IDEATE pass (2026-06-17)

One-page A4 print leave-behind for senior executives. Spine = the intersection of **AI ∩ Customer Value Management ∩ Strategy**. The intersection is the ownable territory; the proof that it is un-copyable is that one operator (Mitch Ribar) has actually run all three at enterprise scale.

Voice: senior, plain-spoken, independent, premium editorial confidence. Short sentences. Named, falsifiable claims. No hype. Ogilvy wins over generic marketing-psych. Banned: "productised", "packaged", "off-the-shelf", "menu", "solutions" (filler), anything SaaS-shaped.

All proof is drawn from the safe-to-use facts in the brief. No invented credentials or numbers. Gated metrics are not used anywhere below; where an achievement is referenced it is qualitative.

---

## 1. Headline / spine expressions (10–14 candidates)

Each expresses the AI ∩ Customer-Value ∩ Strategy intersection as the territory.

1. **"Where AI, customer value, and strategy actually meet."**
   - *Rationale:* States the three circles and their intersection plainly; "actually" implies most claim it, few stand on it.
   - *Risk:* "actually" can read defensive; borders on category-description rather than a promise.

2. **"AI is easy to buy. Customer value is hard to build. We do the second."**
   - *Rationale:* Ogilvy-style contrast; reframes the buyer's real problem away from AI hype toward the LTV outcome.
   - *Risk:* Half-drops "strategy" from the spine; "we" before the proof is established.

3. **"Three disciplines. One operator who has run all three."**
   - *Rationale:* Leads with the un-copyable claim — the intersection plus the single-operator proof in seven words.
   - *Risk:* Doesn't name the three disciplines in the line itself; relies on the sub-head to land them.

4. **"The advisor who has sat in the customer seat, the AI seat, and the strategy seat."**
   - *Rationale:* Concrete, falsifiable, seniority-signalling; "sat in the seat" beats "expert in."
   - *Risk:* Long for a hero line; three-seat rhythm risks listiness.

5. **"AI that grows the customer, not just the deck."**
   - *Rationale:* Sharp, memorable, ties AI to customer-value outcome and quietly mocks consultancy output.
   - *Risk:* "deck" jab may read flip for a board audience; strategy circle implicit only.

6. **"Customer value is the strategy. AI is how you compound it."**
   - *Rationale:* Welds all three circles into one assertion with a clear cause-and-effect; very on-spine.
   - *Risk:* Slightly abstract; "compound" is doing heavy lifting.

7. **"Retention is a strategy problem. We solve it with AI and twenty years in the seat."**
   - *Rationale:* Names the audience's live pain (retention), then the two other circles plus the operator proof.
   - *Risk:* Two sentences, two claims — busy for a hero; "we solve" slightly salesy.

8. **"The intersection of AI, customer value, and strategy — run by someone who has."**
   - *Rationale:* Names the territory and the proof in one breath; "run by someone who has" is a clean dare to competitors.
   - *Risk:* Em-dash construction can read editorial-AI; "who has" hangs without object unless sub-head closes it.

9. **"Most firms pick one. The work lives where all three connect."**
   - *Rationale:* Positions rivals as single-circle; claims the intersection as the place value is actually created.
   - *Risk:* "Most firms" is a generic-consultant move; doesn't name the three until the diagram.

10. **"AI, customer growth, and strategy — held by one set of hands."**
    - *Rationale:* "One set of hands" carries the principal-led, no-analyst-layer promise and the integration claim at once.
    - *Risk:* "held by one set of hands" is slightly poetic for the plainest register.

11. **"He has run the customer base, built the AI, and owned the strategy. Now he advises on all three."**
    - *Rationale:* Pure operator proof; the three verbs map one-to-one to the three circles.
    - *Risk:* Third-person "he" on a flyer can feel like a bio caption, not a headline.

12. **"Where the AI strategy and the customer outcome are the same conversation."**
    - *Rationale:* Captures the integration thesis — these are not three projects but one — in the buyer's language.
    - *Risk:* No proof and no operator in the line; leans entirely on sub-head.

13. **"AI strategy that answers to the customer P&L."**
    - *Rationale:* Ties AI and strategy directly to the CFO/COO's number; falsifiable, senior, unhyped.
    - *Risk:* "P&L" narrows to finance buyers; customer-value circle implied not named.

14. **"Strategy you can run. AI you can trust. Customers who stay."**
    - *Rationale:* Triad maps cleanly to the three circles; "customers who stay" is the plainest possible retention promise.
    - *Risk:* Triad cadence risks sounding like a tagline rather than a claim; "you can trust" is soft.

### Top 3 headlines (with reasoning)

- **#8 — "The intersection of AI, customer value, and strategy — run by someone who has."**
  Names the exact territory and folds the un-copyable operator proof into the same line. It is the spine, stated. The em-dash is the one risk to watch in build (could swap to a period: "…and strategy. Run by someone who has.").

- **#3 — "Three disciplines. One operator who has run all three."**
  The shortest, most quotable expression of the moat. Pairs naturally with the three-circle diagram (which supplies the names the line withholds) and a CV sub-head. Highest memorability of the set.

- **#13 — "AI strategy that answers to the customer P&L."**
  The most falsifiable and most CFO-native line; least like a tagline, most like a promise. Best where the flyer must survive a finance buyer's skepticism. Pair with a sub-head that restores the customer-value circle by name.

*Uncertain:* whether the buyer reads "customer value" as a term of art or as vague. The diagram and sub-head must carry the definition (lifecycle / retention / growth / LTV) so the headline can stay tight.

### Top 2

- **#4 — "The person who scopes the engagement has personally run customer engagement at Optus, AI personalization at Chase, and a SaaS P&L from MVP to scale."**
  Best marries the un-copyable proof to the operating-model promise (principal-led, no analyst layer). Names two safe employers, maps to all three circles, stays plain. "from MVP to scale" carries the AI-program scale fact without a gated number.

- **#2 — "He recovered Optus's customer base after a national outage and a cyberattack, ran AI personalization across 50M+ Chase customers, and built Quantium's financial-services data engine. Same person. All three circles."**
  Most vivid and most falsifiable. The Optus crisis recovery is the single most memorable, least-copyable proof point on the CV; "Same person. All three circles." nails the thesis. Slightly longer — trim if the diagram already labels the circles.

*Note:* The "served half the Fortune 100" and "Chase serves ~50% of US households" facts are strong but are company facts, not his personal achievement — better placed as a small credibility footnote than in the spine sub-head, to avoid implying he personally owns them.

## 3. Method re-wordings (6–8 full 5-step sets)

True to: challenge requirements → remove process → optimize → accelerate cycle time → automate. Re-worded distinctly from Musk ("question requirements, delete, simplify, accelerate cycle time, automate"). Scored 1–5 on **distinctness-from-Musk (D)**, **operator-credibility (C)**, **memorability (M)**.

**Set A — "Interrogate / Strip / Sharpen / Compress / Hand off"**
1. **Interrogate** — challenge every requirement on the table
2. **Strip** — cut the process that earns nothing
3. **Sharpen** — make what remains the best it can be
4. **Compress** — take time out of the cycle
5. **Hand off** — automate only what's already proven
- D 4 / C 4 / M 4. "Hand off" reframes automate as delegation-after-proof — operator instinct, not Musk's "automate last."

**Set B — "Question / Cut / Refine / Quicken / Automate"**
1. **Question** — pressure-test the requirement before the build
2. **Cut** — remove the steps no one would miss
3. **Refine** — optimize what survives the cut
4. **Quicken** — shorten the loop from decision to result
5. **Automate** — only the part that's stable and proven
- D 2 / C 4 / M 3. Too close to Musk on words 1 and 5; safe but echoes.

**Set C — "Challenge / Delete / Tune / Speed / Systemize"**
1. **Challenge** — is this requirement real, or inherited?
2. **Delete** — remove the process, not just trim it
3. **Tune** — optimize for the outcome that pays
4. **Speed** — compress the cycle time
5. **Systemize** — automate the proven path
- D 2 / C 4 / M 3. "Delete" is Musk's exact word; drop.

**Set D — "Pressure-test / Subtract / Optimize / Accelerate / Automate"**
1. **Pressure-test** — challenge the requirement at the source
2. **Subtract** — remove process before adding anything
3. **Optimize** — improve what's left, in that order
4. **Accelerate** — take time out of the cycle
5. **Automate** — last, and only what's earned it
- D 3 / C 5 / M 3. Highest credibility (this is how operators actually talk: "subtract before you add", "automate last"), but "accelerate/automate" still rhyme with Musk.

**Set E — "Doubt it / Drop it / Dial it / Drive it / Delegate it"**
1. **Doubt it** — challenge the requirement
2. **Drop it** — remove the process
3. **Dial it** — optimize the rest
4. **Drive it** — compress cycle time
5. **Delegate it** — automate what's proven
- D 4 / C 3 / M 5. Maximally memorable (D-alliteration, "-it" rhythm), and "delegate" cleverly reframes automate. Risk: the cadence flirts with cute, which the register polices.

**Set F — "Justify / Remove / Improve / Shorten / Automate"**
1. **Justify** — every requirement earns its place or goes
2. **Remove** — take out the process, not the people
3. **Improve** — optimize the work that remains
4. **Shorten** — compress the cycle time
5. **Automate** — only the proven, repeatable path
- D 3 / C 5 / M 2. Very credible and plain; least memorable (generic verbs).

**Set G — "Challenge the brief / Remove the process / Optimize the work / Compress the cycle / Automate the proven"**
1. **Challenge the brief** — is the requirement real?
2. **Remove the process** — delete before you optimize
3. **Optimize the work** — sharpen what survives
4. **Compress the cycle** — take out the time, not the rigor
5. **Automate the proven** — last, never first
- D 3 / C 5 / M 4. The noun-anchored phrasing reads most senior and is self-explaining on a flyer; "Automate the proven" is a strong, distinctive close.

### Top set + runner-up

- **TOP — Set G ("Challenge the brief / Remove the process / Optimize the work / Compress the cycle / Automate the proven").**
  Best balance for a *print flyer read in under a minute*: each step is self-explaining without the gloss, the verbs are operator-plain, and "delete before you optimize" / "automate the proven, never first" are genuinely distinct operating opinions — not Musk paraphrase, not consultant fluff. Sequence-as-opinion is the differentiator: the order itself is the claim.

- **RUNNER-UP — Set D ("Pressure-test / Subtract / Optimize / Accelerate / Automate").**
  Highest pure operator-credibility; "subtract before you add" and "automate last, only what's earned it" are the lines a COO trusts. Use if the build wants single-word step labels over Set G's phrases.

*If maximum memorability is wanted over seniority:* Set E (the "-it" set), accepting the cuteness risk.

### Method names (must not echo "the algorithm")

1. **"The Operating Method."** — Plainest, most senior, hardest to mock; "operating" ties to operator. *Recommended.*
2. **"The Five Cuts."** — Memorable, implies subtraction (the spine of the method); slightly aggressive, which suits the audience.
3. **"The Sequence."** — Leans on the method's real differentiator (order is the opinion); risks vagueness without the steps beside it.

*Recommended name: "The Operating Method"* (with "The Five Cuts" as the punchier alternative if the flyer wants a hook).

## 5. Surprising / non-obvious differentiation angles (steelmanned)

**A. Lead with the crisis-recovery story, not the intersection diagram.**
The single least-copyable, most emotionally resonant fact on the CV is that he *recovered Optus's customer base after a national outage and a cyberattack.* Every regulated, retention-driven board lives in fear of exactly that event.
- *Why strong:* It is the proof that he operates under real stakes, not slideware. It is unforgeable — almost no advisor has done it. It makes "customer value management" visceral instead of academic.
- *Why it backfires:* Optus may be sensitive about public framing; foregrounding someone else's crisis can read as opportunistic; it narrows the perceived offer to "turnaround/crisis" when the spine is broader. Use as proof, not as the lead claim.

**B. Make the method's *sequence* the differentiator, not the steps.**
Anyone can list five verbs. The defensible, non-Musk move is to assert the *order as an opinion*: remove process **before** you optimize; automate **last**, never first. The order is a falsifiable operating belief most consultants get backwards (they automate the mess).
- *Why strong:* It sidesteps the Musk-echo problem entirely — you're not competing on the verbs, you're competing on the sequence-logic, which is genuinely his. It is the most "operator, not consultant" thing on the page.
- *Why it backfires:* Requires one extra sentence of explanation; a scanning reader may miss the sequence-claim and just see five verbs. Mitigate with a single line: "The order is the method."

**C. Position AI as the *least* interesting circle — deliberately.**
Everyone is shouting "AI." The contrarian premium move is to treat AI as table-stakes and make *customer value* and *strategy* the scarce circles. "Plenty of people can build the model. Almost no one can tell you which customer outcome it should change."
- *Why strong:* It is counter-cyclical to 2026 AI-slop, signals seniority (only the secure dismiss the hype), and is true to the spine — the intersection, not the AI, is the territory.
- *Why it backfires:* The audience may have an explicit "AI strategy" mandate from the board and want AI foregrounded; under-selling AI could mis-cue the buyer about scope. Calibrate: demote the *hype*, not the *capability*.

**D. Name the analyst-layer absence as the product.**
The sharpest contrast with big-four isn't seniority-in-the-abstract — it's the concrete promise that *no one you didn't meet will touch the work.* "The principal who scopes it signs it." This is an operating-model differentiator, not a credential.
- *Why strong:* It's falsifiable, it's the buyer's real fear (paying partner rates for analyst work), and it's structurally un-copyable by a firm that runs on leverage. A big-four firm *cannot* make this promise without breaking its economics.
- *Why it backfires:* It can read as "small / no team / can't scale," which a large engagement may want. Frame as deliberate model, not capacity limit.

**E. Use scale-asymmetry as the close: enterprise scars, at your scale.**
The proof points are 50M–90M+ customers and 300M events/day. Most buyers are smaller. The non-obvious framing: "Problems you're hitting for the first time, he has already solved at 10x your scale."
- *Why strong:* Converts intimidating big numbers from "irrelevant to me" into "de-risking for me" — the scale becomes a *safety* argument, not a flex. Strong for mid-market regulated firms.
- *Why it backfires:* If overdone it implies the buyer is small; and very large buyers may read it as condescending. Tune the multiplier language to the segment.

*Strongest single angle: **B (sequence-as-the-differentiator).*** It is the only one that simultaneously solves a locked constraint (kill the Musk echo), reinforces the spine (operator judgment, not tooling), and is fully owned by the founder. A and D are the strongest *proof* angles; B is the strongest *positioning* angle.

## 6. What to avoid (flyer-specific traps)

- **The AI-slop tells.** No display-serif drop-caps, no tiny tracked uppercase eyebrow over every block, no numbered section markers as default scaffolding. The method's 1–5 numbering is the *only* place numerals-as-structure are earned.
- **Three circles drawn as a generic Venn clip-art.** The diagram must look art-directed (type-led, restrained), not like a PowerPoint smart-graphic — that single element can sink the whole "senior" impression.
- **Banned words creep.** Re-grep the final copy for "productised", "packaged", "off-the-shelf", "menu", and filler "solutions." The method's verbs and the proof lines are where these sneak in.
- **Gated numbers.** Do not let a build step "strengthen" a line by adding 310%, $22B LTV, $110M, -12% OpEx, $60M, basis points, or a founding year. Every metric on the page must be from the safe list. Flag any number a reviewer adds.
- **Musk-echo regression.** If anyone "tightens" the method back toward "question / delete / simplify / accelerate / automate," the distinctness is lost. Hold Set G (or D).
- **Reusing the site's signature line verbatim.** "Operators, not consultants." belongs to the site's kinetic phrase; echo its spirit, generate fresh on the flyer.
- **Over-claiming company facts as personal.** "Served half the Fortune 100" (Brand Networks) and "~50% of US households" (Chase) are *company* facts — never phrase them as his personal achievement. Footnote-tier, not spine.
- **Crowding the A4.** One headline, one sub-head, one diagram, one method, one proof strip, one CTA. The premium signal is restraint; whitespace is the budget the design can least afford to spend.
- **CTA softness.** The page sells senior advisory engagements (from $35,000; bespoke on request). The flyer's CTA should be a discovery call, plainly stated — not "learn more," not a SaaS-shaped "get started."
